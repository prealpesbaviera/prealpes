The Mountain of Bruno the Bear.

Before setting off, we decided with Eva's uncle Gerhard to head over to Austria, since the weather was beautiful. Summer was coming to an end, the days were just about perfect for this kind of activity, and little by little I was feeling the urge to climb more and more mountains. Besides, I was expecting a very fit visitor from Colombia soon, and we were planning to conquer several summits together. I couldn't afford to embarrass myself with my physical condition, so I thought: well, why not? This looks interesting, I don't need any particularly intense training beforehand, it's never too late to get started, and the only person you're competing against is yourself—if the word competing even makes sense here. These thoughts kept running through my head throughout the day, and off I went hiking.

This wasn't my first mountain, but it was the first in a series of 100 hiking routes. There isn't really a clear rule defining what counts as a summit, what counts as a mountain, or what is simply a gentle walk. My challenge is about getting out there and immersing myself in nature, living by its rules—the ones that truly matter—and leaving all that urban life behind. During autumn and winter, for safety reasons, considering the snow accumulation and shorter days, most of the summits I tackle will be between 1,000 and 2,000 metres. Once spring is over, I want to take on mountains between 2,000 and 3,000 metres above sea level. The ultimate challenge will be the Zugspitze, Germany's highest mountain: reaching the summit and returning in a single day, around twelve hours of demanding hiking. For now, though, I'll start relatively gently and gradually increase the difficulty. This first mountain is officially rated moderate, although it's certainly demanding for an ordinary person. It wasn't exactly the most suitable choice for a beginner, but I wanted to start with an iconic mountain, and what better place than Bärenkopf—the Bear's Head?

Who was Bruno the Bear?

This story begins at the Museum of Man and Nature in Munich, where the poor bear's stuffed body is on display. I went there last year with Eva and my son Jan, and his story stayed with us long afterwards. For almost 170 years, no wild bear had been seen roaming freely in Bavaria. Then, all of a sudden, one appeared and began wandering around near populated areas as if it were the most normal thing in the world. Naturally, he became a media sensation throughout the region, sparking heated controversy between those who saw him as a threat and those determined to protect him. Bruno wandered through many of the mountain areas I plan to explore during this challenge, and according to local stories, he was particularly fond of the area around today's destination. That's why Bärenkopf is sometimes unofficially called the mountain of Bruno the Bear.

As happens with so many species, his only real crime was simply existing. His parents had been brought to the Italian Alps as part of a European Union programme called LIFE Ursus, aimed at restoring the bear population in the region. Bruno was born there, and at the age of two, apparently tired of living with his parents, he decided it was time to get his own place. So off he went, roaming freely through South Tyrol and eventually making his way into southern Bavaria. When it came to finding food, Bruno discovered a rather convenient solution: eating whatever farm animals happened to cross his path. Unsurprisingly, it didn't take long for much of the region's farming community to turn against him, with some people even organising to hunt him down illegally. This was May 2006, just before Germany hosted the FIFA World Cup, when television cameras from around the world were focused on the country and security measures had to be particularly strict. Bruno's adventures suddenly became an international media story, at times even stealing newspaper headlines from Germany's national football team.

Of course, when a bear gets too close to human settlements, something has to be done, because sooner or later it could attack someone. To avoid a scandal under the eyes of the international media, the authorities initially decided to capture Bruno alive and relocate him to a sanctuary abroad. They tried a bit of everything, but nothing worked. They even brought in specialist hunters from Finland. According to a joke circulating among the locals, however, the Finnish experts spent more time sampling every variety of Bavarian beer than actually hunting for the bear—all at the government's expense. Eventually, tired of the endless back-and-forth and concerned about the possible danger to World Cup tourists, Bavaria's environment minister authorised Bruno to be shot. A few hours later, on 26 June 2006, local hunters killed him. The decision and how quickly it was carried out triggered an enormous public outcry. To this day, the hunters' identities remain secret because some people wanted to do to them exactly what they had done to Bruno. Anyway, his memory lives on, and for me it was a real pleasure to walk through a landscape that had once been part of his habitat.

The Route to the Summit

Uncle Gerhard has a particular fondness for shortcuts. His reasoning is simple: the shortest distance between two points, A and B, is always a straight line. So naturally, we headed straight up the ski slope. The mathematician who came up with that principle—Euclid—must have spent his entire life sitting behind a desk. I seriously doubt he ever climbed a bloody great mountain. Like any respectable ski slope, this one was ridiculously steep, and within fifteen minutes we were on the verge of leaving our bodies, souls and everything else behind. We still had more than a thousand metres of elevation to gain, and I already felt as though I'd used up half my battery. Fortunately, Eva protested, and we found the proper hiking trail.

The main part of the route led towards a mountain pasture called Bärenbadalm, which roughly translates as the Bears' Bath Alpine Pasture. An Alm is traditionally a high-altitude grazing area, usually with one or more Alpine huts. Some huts serve food, and others also offer accommodation. A classic Alpine outing involves making your way up to one of these places and enjoying traditional food surrounded by spectacular mountain scenery. In the case of Bärenkopf, you can also use a cable car to cover much of the climb. That's why my parents-in-law joined us at the Alm that day. We had arranged to meet them there later. The summit, however, was still much higher up. At Bärenbadalm, we were roughly halfway up the mountain. From there, you can reach the summit as part of a circular route. We chose the loop that begins behind the Alm, offering the views you can see in this photograph.

Because she was exhausted, and because we had arranged to meet my parents-in-law, Eva decided to skip the final ten percent of the climb. She completed the loop and returned to the Alm without reaching the summit. Gerhard and I carried on. I was every bit as exhausted as Eva, but there was no way I was going to miss that view. And listen, if you ever find yourself at Achensee—the lake you can see down below—don't hesitate for a second: get yourself up that mountain! The climb is absolutely worth it. You're right in the heart of the Alps. What more could you possibly ask for?

A Little Something to Eat at the Alm, Then Back Down We Go

After enjoying that incredible panorama from the summit, we hurried back down because my parents-in-law were already waiting at the Alm, and patience isn't exactly their strongest quality. I'd never descended a mountain so quickly in my life. I think it qualified as power hiking, because in just forty minutes we dropped 500 metres along a steep, root-covered trail. By then, I'd completely exhausted my water supplies, and when I finally reached the Alm, my brain had switched into full Homer Simpson mode and could only produce one word: beer. I drank an entire litre in practically no time. Then I had Kaiserschmarrn, the traditional Austrian shredded pancake, together with Eva, Gerhard and my parents-in-law. It was a lovely moment, sitting there with people who mean so much to me.

Afterwards, my parents-in-law headed towards the cable car, while the rest of us continued downhill. One funny thing about climbing mountains is that you lose all sense of how far you've actually climbed, and you tend to forget that every metre you go up is another metre you have to come back down. We still had another 500 metres of descent ahead of us. That day I learned one of the fundamental rules of hiking: a beer at the end of a hike? Absolutely. But make sure it's really the end! That litre I'd enjoyed at the Alm came back to haunt me. The first third of the remaining descent was particularly difficult. My legs felt incredibly heavy, and I had absolutely no enthusiasm left for walking downhill. After a while, though, my body got moving again, and I managed to continue more or less normally.

At last, we reached the car park beside the lake. Now picture this: a lake straight out of a film, a beautiful sunny day, and a tremendous mountain hike behind you. What's the first thing that comes to mind? Into the water, of course! There was just one small problem. After several cold days, the weather had finally turned relatively warm—at least by our standards—and it hadn't occurred to us to bring swimwear. So we started by dipping our feet into the water, but it felt so good that our legs kept going deeper and deeper. Eventually we looked at each other and said, "Oh, what the hell. Let's just swim in our underwear!" Swimming in that lake was the ultimate reward, the ribbon on a gift from nature after a perfect day. Finally, we headed home, without any underwear beneath our outer clothes, but incredibly happy and grateful for the wonderful day we'd had.

In memory of Bruno.
